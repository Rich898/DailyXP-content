## GEOGRAPHY

### Liveability
- "Liveability refers to the quality of life experienced by people in a place, based on environmental, social, and economic factors."
- Indicators (diagram, Lesson 2.2):
  - DENSITY – Maximizing compact urban form while mitigating negative aspects such as air pollution and congestion
  - DIVERSITY OF USE AND INCOME – Neighborhoods with mixed income groups and diverse opportunities for jobs, commerce, and leisure
  - DESIGN – Shaping cities so that urban residents benefit from the advantages of dense areas. Good design includes walkability, traffic safety controls, and tree cover.
  - DISTANCE TO TRANSIT – Transit options should ideally be accessible within 400-800m
  - DESTINATION ACCESS – Sustainable transportation modes that take people where they want to go
- Liveability is judged with social, economic and environmental indicators.

### Key urban processes (Lesson 1.2)
- Economic restructuring: "is the transformation of an economy's industries and employment usually involving the decline of some sectors and the growth of others. For example, a shift from manufacturing towards service and knowledge-based industries."
- Urban consolidation: "The process of increasing population density within existing urban areas through higher-density housing, such as apartments, townhouses and infill development, to reduce urban sprawl and make more efficient use of existing infrastructure."
  - Short version: "Building more housing (like apartments or townhouses) within existing suburbs to use land, transport, and services more efficiently."
- Urban renewal: "The process of redeveloping or revitalising older, deteriorating or underused urban areas to improve their economic, social and environmental functioning."
- Urban sprawl [TEXTBOOK – not from school]: the spreading outwards of a city into surrounding rural or natural land, usually through low-density housing on the city's edge, increasing car dependence and infrastructure costs.
- Population density [TEXTBOOK – not from school]: the number of people (or dwellings) per unit of area, e.g. people per km² or dwellings per hectare. Dee Why went from 35 to 65 dwellings/ha.
- Gentrification: "the process by which higher-income residents and businesses move into previously lower-income urban areas, raising property values and transforming the social character of the place."
- Infrastructure: "the set of facilities and systems that serve a country, city, or other area and encompasses the services and facilities necessary for its economy, households and businesses to function."
- Urbanisation: "An increasing proportion of a country's population lives in urban areas." Urban growth: "An increase in the number of people living in urban areas." Megacity: "An urban area with 10 million+ people."

### Element vs compound [TEXTBOOK – not from school]
- Element: a pure substance made of only one type of atom. It cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means (e.g. oxygen O₂, iron Fe).
- Compound: a pure substance made of two or more different elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio (e.g. water H₂O, carbon dioxide CO₂). It can be broken down by chemical reactions.
- School link (Dalton): "Compounds are formed by combinations of two or more different types of atoms." "Atoms combine in simple, whole-number ratios to form compounds. Like H₂O (2 hydrogens : 1 oxygen)."

### Physical vs chemical change
- Physical change: "Change in state / Change in size / Change in shape / Maintains chemical composition". "A change that does not involve a chemical reaction. The form of the substance changes but the substance remains the same."
- Chemical reaction: "A process in which one or more substances, the reactants, are converted to one or more new substances, the products."
- 4 indicators of chemical change: "Colour change; Formation of a precipitate (often seen as a solution going cloudy...); Formation of a gas/odour; Temperature change"
- Examples: burning paper (chemical), boiling an egg (chemical), boiling water (physical), breaking a cookie (physical), burning a match (chemical), shredding paper (physical).
- Reactant: "A substance that is consumed in the course of a chemical reaction." Product: "A substance that is produced as a result of a chemical reaction."
- Law of conservation of mass: "Total mass of reactants = total mass of the products." "...in a chemical reaction mass cannot be created or destroyed."
- Open system: "allows the transfer of matter in or out of the system." Closed system: "does not allow transfer of matter in or out of the system."

### Acid + carbonate
- School practical: vinegar (acid, CH₃COOH) + bicarbonate of soda (carbonate, NaHCO₃) produces carbon dioxide gas. In an open beaker the CO₂ escapes, so mass decreases.
- General rule [TEXTBOOK]: acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
- CO₂ test 1: "Place a burning splint next to the mouth of test tube. If the splint is extinguished then carbon dioxide is the gas produced."
- CO₂ test 2: "Bubbling gas into limewater. Limewater changes from clear to cloudy if carbon dioxide gas is present."
- Heating a carbonate: CuCO₃(s) → CuO(s) + CO₂(g), a decomposition reaction.

- evaporate: "To change state from a liquid to a gas at a slow rate."
- boil: "To change state from a liquid to a gas at a rapid rate."
- melt: "To change state from a solid to a liquid."
- freeze: "To change state from a liquid to a solid."
- sublimation: "...directly from a solid to a gas without going through a liquid phase."
- deposition: "...directly from a gas to a solid without going through a liquid phase."

### Distillation and separating mixtures (Mixtures Quizlet)
- distillation: "A process that separates solutions on the basis of their different boiling points." [TEXTBOOK addition: the liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates first, then condenses back to a liquid in a cooled condenser and is collected.]
- pure substance: "A substance made up of the same type of particle."
- mixture: "A substance made up of different types of particles in no fixed ratio. Can be physically separated."
- filtration: "A process used to separate suspensions using filter paper."
- evaporation (separation): "A process in which the solvent changes from a liquid to a gas leaving the solute."
- crystallisation: "...a solution is cooled to such an extent that the solute begins to form crystals and separates from the solution."
- chromatography: "Dissolved solutes and their solvent moving at different rates through a medium."

### Hypothesis and variables
- Hypothesis: "This is an educated guess of what you think will happen."
  - Model format: "If the distance between fruits is decreased, the ripening rate will increase/decrease/stay the same." (i.e. If [IV changes], then [DV will...])
- Independent variable: "the factor that is changed by the experimenter during a scientific investigation."
- Dependent variable: "the factor being tested or measured during a scientific investigation, it only changes because it depends on the independent variable."
- Controlled variable: "a factor that is kept the same during a scientific investigation. There are many controlled variables in an investigation."
  - Example (hair gel): IV = type of gel; DV = how well it holds; CVs = amount of gel, time left in, type of hair, cleanliness of hair.
  - Example (apples): CVs = "Same type of apple, same mass of each apple, same conditions, same amount of time, same iodine solution"
- Reliability: repeat the experiment at least 3 times. Validity: is it a fair test? Limitations: "factors that are unable to be controlled or assumptions that are made during measurement".
- Describing trends: always mention both variables.
- Video: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2606GQmDqY

### Other chemistry (also on the exam)
- Synthesis: "two or more reactants combine to form a single product. A + B → AB"
- Decomposition: "break down a large molecule and turn it into two or more smaller molecules... AB → A + B". Example: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂
- Corrosion: "The degradation (or eating away) of metal so that it loses strength and becomes unable to fulfil its intended purpose." "Metal + Water + Oxygen → Hydrated Metal Oxide". It is a synthesis reaction and needs all 3.
  - Prevention: painting; oil, grease or tar; plastic coating; galvanising ("zinc is more reactive than iron").
- Precipitation: "two soluble salts combine and one of the products is an insoluble salt called a precipitate. AB + CD → AD + CB"
  - Soluble: nitrates, sulfates. Insoluble: hydroxides, carbonates, phosphates.
- Ionic bonding: "between a metal and non metal and involve the transfer of electrons". Covalent bonding: "between two nonmetals and involve the sharing of electrons". Metallic bonding: "a lattice of positively charged metal nuclei in a sea of delocalised electrons".
- Balancing equations: "Only the number out the front of a substance can be changed, the subscript numbers cannot be changed."

## COMMERCE (Yearly exam topics)
Exam format: gapfill on the 5 sectors, 3 leakages, 3 injections and the business cycle. Short answers on circular flow, inflation, monetary policy and fiscal policy.

### Scarcity
"Refers to the economic problem where human wants and needs are unlimited, but the available resources to satisfy them are finite. Involving making choices, facing trade-offs, and calculating opportunity cost (the value of the next-best alternative you give up when you make a choice). For example: Having a limited $20 budget and having to choose between buying a new t-shirt or going to see a movie."

### Opportunity cost
"Refers to: The hidden 'price' or trade-off of selecting one option over another. Involving: Finite or scarce resources, commonly time, money, and effort. For example: If you decide to spend an hour studying for an exam instead of watching your favorite TV show, your opportunity cost is the enjoyment and relaxation you missed out on."
- Model answer: "The government chooses the hospital upgrade. The railway extension is its next-best feasible alternative. Therefore, the opportunity cost is the railway extension and the transport benefits it could provide."
- Note: there is always an opportunity cost, even when choosing the best option.

### GDP ("This is in the test!")
"Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the total monetary value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a given period. It involves measuring final goods, such as new cars and bread, and services, such as haircuts and healthcare. It allows economists to compare economic output across countries and track changes over time."
- Economic growth = an increase in a nation's level of production and income.

### Circular flow of income (diagram will be in the yearly exam)
- 5 SECTORS: Households, Businesses (Firms), Financial, Government, Overseas
- Households → Firms: consumer expenditure (E) and labour. Firms → Households: income/wages (Y) and goods & services.
- 3 LEAKAGES: Savings (S) → Financial; Taxation (T) → Government; Imports (M) → Overseas
- 3 INJECTIONS: Investment (I) ← Financial; Government expenditure (G) ← Government; Exports (X) ← Overseas
- Injections: "Introduction of income into the flow." Leakages: "Withdrawal of income from the flow."
- Tax: "A compulsory contribution to state revenue." Factors of production: "Resources that are used to produce goods and services."

### Fiscal policy (Government)
- "Fiscal policy is the use of government spending and taxation to influence economic activity."
- Aims: "(i) Supply public goods that contribute to the welfare of citizens and promote economic growth (ii) Help stabilise the cyclical fluctuations of the economy."
- "Expansionary fiscal policy: stimulates economic activity by increasing government spending and/or decreasing taxation." "Contractionary fiscal policy: reduces economic activity by increasing taxation and/or decreasing government spending."
- Chain: "Income tax decreases → disposable income increases → households may spend more → businesses receive more revenue → businesses may increase production and employment."
- Budget surplus = revenue > expenditure. Deficit = revenue < expenditure. Balanced = revenue = expenditure.

### Monetary policy (Reserve Bank) and interest rates
- "The RBA is Australia's central bank. It sets the cash rate, which influences the interest rates banks charge on loans and pay on savings... helping the RBA keep inflation low and stable and support employment."
- "monetary policy involves influencing interest rates to affect demand, employment and inflation in the economy. Done through movement of the cash rate."
- Aims: "(i) Promote economic prosperity and welfare (ii) Maintain price stability (iii) Help stabilise the cyclical fluctuations of the economy by promoting full employment."
- Rate RISE: "RBA raises the cash rate → borrowing interest rates generally rise → some households and businesses cut back or delay purchases → demand grows more slowly → inflation pressure may ease over time."
- Rate CUT: borrowing becomes cheaper, so spending and investment increase. For mortgage holders, lower repayments mean more disposable income (e.g. a new kitchen, which creates jobs).
- Difference: "Fiscal policy works through government spending and taxation. Monetary policy works mainly through the RBA's cash rate target and its influence on interest rates."
- Exam scaffold: "Monetary policy refers to... It aims to... It impacts the circular flow of income by... For example..."

## FOOD TECHNOLOGY

### Temperature danger zone (school sample answer)
- Is... "the temperature range between 5°C and 60°C where dangerous bacteria grows and multiplies."
- Affects... "perishable foods including raw meat and dairy products that are left in this temperature range because the food can become unsafe to eat due to the high levels of bacteria."
- Causes... "food to become unsafe to eat and can result in food poisoning. Symptoms include vomiting, diarrhoea, headaches and even death."
- Avoided by... "storing perishable food such as raw meat and dairy products in the fridge at 4°C or below."
- Example: "Food can enter the danger zone if a fridge is faulty and isn't storing food below 4°C. Perishable food (ham/raw chicken) is stored on the kitchen bench at room temperature for too long."

### Three types of food product development (textbook Ch 3)
- NEW TO THE WORLD – "A brand-new innovative idea". "...the result of completely new ideas. These may be a new concept in packaging, such as the packaging for Turkish Delight Tim Tams that changes colour when refrigerated, or a product directed to a new target market, such as cholesterol-lowering Weet-Bix or foods for consumers with a gluten intolerance." It is very expensive and risky.
- LINE EXTENSION – "A new version of a product already available". "...the development of a new flavour, or a recipe revision that changes the recipe to alter its nutritive value, or to make it low in salt or high in fibre... a safer, lower-cost way of attracting customers, [but] produce less profit than a brand-new innovation."
  - Examples: gluten-free cereals, Fanta Jellyfizz, Powerade with coconut water. The Shapes 2016 backlash is an example of a line extension backfiring.
- ME-TOO – "A product similar to another product already in the market". "...aimed at attracting customers for an already established and successful product by creating a similar product. Often companies will copy both the competitor's product and the packaging style; however, the profits and market share of the copy are rarely threatening to the original."

### Functions of nutrients (textbook Ch 4)
- "Nutrients are the chemical substances in food that our bodies process to enable it to function." There are six: proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, vitamins, minerals, water.
- Three functions of food: growth and development; provision of energy; repair and maintenance of cells.
- PROTEINS: build and repair hard and soft body tissue (bone, teeth, muscle, organs); make enzymes, antibodies, hormones, haemoglobin; provide energy (17 kJ/g) when no other source is available.
- CARBOHYDRATES: provide energy (17 kJ/g). Simple = sugars; complex = starches.
- LIPIDS: energy (37 kJ/g); warmth and protection; transport of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, K; omega-6 for cell walls and omega-3 for brain development.
- VITAMINS: "protect the body against disease and regulate body processes."
  - A: growth, healthy skin and eyes
  - B1–B3: obtain energy from food
  - B9 folate: prevents neural tube defects in babies
  - C: healthy skin, bones and muscles; resistance to infection; helps absorb iron
  - D: strong bones and teeth (helps absorb calcium)
- MINERALS: "protect the body against disease and regulate body processes" and are needed "in very small amounts".
  - Calcium: with phosphorus and vitamin D, strengthens bones and teeth
  - Iron: forms haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood
  - Sodium: maintains fluid and electrolyte levels; muscle contraction
- WATER: regulates processes such as digestion and respiration. The body is about 70% water; we need about 2 L a day.

### Micronutrients [TEXTBOOK – not from school]
Micronutrients are nutrients the body needs in only very small amounts, namely vitamins and minerals (e.g. vitamin C, iron, calcium). Macronutrients are needed in large amounts and provide energy: carbohydrates, proteins and lipids (water is also needed in large amounts).

### Bush foods (textbook Ch 1)
- "'bush tucker' in fact describes the diverse range of animals, like kangaroo, and plants – including berries, nuts and herbs – native to Australia." Examples: lemon myrtle, wattleseed, lilly pilly, strawberry gum, pepper berry, midgen berry.
- Traditional diet: "rich and nutritionally well balanced... high in protein... high in fibre and micronutrients... small amount of sugar... intake of fat was low". Bush foods are now seen as "superfoods".
- Kakadu plum: "100 times more vitamin C than an orange and 5.2 times more antioxidants than a blueberry"
- Quandong: twice the vitamin C of an orange
- Finger lime: three times the vitamin C of a mandarin
- Davidson's plum: potassium, vitamin E, zinc; a dairy-free calcium source
- Wattleseed: protein, low GI
- Kangaroo: lean and high in iron

### Key formulas [TEXTBOOK – standard Stage 5.1 formulas, not copied from school]
- Simple interest: I = P × r × n (P = principal, r = rate per period as a decimal, n = number of periods)
- Percentage change: (new − old) ÷ old × 100
- Midpoint: M = ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2)
- Gradient: m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁) = rise/run
- Distance: d = √((x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²)
- Straight line: y = mx + c (m = gradient, c = y-intercept)
- Index laws: aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ; aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ; (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ; a⁰ = 1
- Pythagoras: c² = a² + b² (c = hypotenuse)

### The Importance of Being Earnest: Yearly Exam Cheat Sheet (verbatim, Glover)
**Vocabulary:** Bunburyist, Eligible, Victorian, Ward, Set Up and Payoff, Lineage, Values, Illegitimate, Scandal, Farce, Vulgar, Denouement, Impropriety, Conceited, Earnest, Clergyman, Dramatic Irony, Christening, Reproach, Governess, Infatuation, Engagement

**Victorian Context / themes:** Identity (Bunburyism); Appearance (Performative society); Gender roles; Social class; Social institutions (Church, Marriage, Education)

### Big idea (9ENGB page, verbatim)
"Life in Victorian society is performative. Wilde uses satire to show that identity, social status, and gender are not natural or authentic, but roles people learn to perform in order to be accepted. Characters adopt false names, pursue appearances over happiness, and act according to rigid gender expectations rather than personal truth."

### What does Wilde satirise? (slides, verbatim)
- Victorian society context: "Victorian society (1837–1901) was built on strong ideas about order, respectability, and social hierarchy... Public behaviour was expected to appear moral, disciplined, and respectable, regardless of private realities. Maintaining appearances was often considered more important than honesty. Thus, Victorian society was incredibly performative."
- Class: "Social class was rigid and highly visible... Marriage was a key institution for preserving class boundaries, wealth, and reputation rather than purely for romantic love."
- Gender: "Men were associated with authority, rationality, and public life, while women were expected to embody purity, obedience, and domestic responsibility."
- Thesis: "Wilde's critiques of Victorian society, through his use of satire, conveys the notion that life, in its many facets; identity, status, and gender, are preordained expectations instilled by the affluent hierarchy that must be satisfied in order to achieve acceptance. Thus, Wilde's ultimate critique of Victorian society is that life is performative; the only means to be vindicated by the higher classes is to conform to their expectations."
- **Identity (Bunburyism):** "Through the concept of Bunburyism, Wilde satirises the idea that identity in Victorian society is flexible, performative, and often dishonest. Characters construct alternate selves in order to escape social obligations, suggesting that sincerity is less valued than convenience and appearance. The repeated confusion between being 'Ernest' and being earnest highlights how identity is reduced to labels rather than moral behaviour."
  - Quote: "Well, my name is Ernest in town and Jack in the country"
- **Status (appearance over happiness):** "Wilde uses marriage as a satirical lens to critique the rigid class structures of Victorian England. Romantic love is consistently subordinated to wealth, lineage, and social respectability, particularly through Lady Bracknell's transactional view of relationships."
  - Quote: "An engagement should come on a young girl as a surprise, pleasant or unpleasant, as the case may be. It is hardly a matter that she could be allowed to arrange for herself"
- **Gender roles:** "Through satire and parody, Wilde subverts Victorian gender expectations by presenting masculinity and femininity as exaggerated and inconsistent performances... exposes them as artificial constructs maintained through repetition and performance."
  - Quote: "The home seems to me to be the proper sphere for the man... once a man begins to neglect his domestic duties he becomes painfully effeminate"
- Other quote: "I am sick to death of cleverness. Everybody is clever nowadays."

## HISTORY (Term 4: World War II – The War in the Pacific)

### Pearl Harbour (7 Dec 1941)
- Japan attacked the US naval base at Pearl Harbour (Hawaii) on 7 December 1941.
- Lesson goals: factors leading to the attack; political and economic causes; consequences for Japan and the USA.
- Exam-style prompt: "For Japan the benefits of the attack on Pearl Harbour were worth the potential consequences"

### Fall of Singapore (Feb 1942)
- Singapore was a major British base that fell to Japan in February 1942. It "shattered Australia's security and shifted its geopolitical alliances toward the United States."
- Propaganda posters: "He's Coming South", "Ringed with Menace"
- Prompt: "Explain how the threat of Japanese expansion changed Australia's sense of security in 1942, and analyze how the government used propaganda posters to shape public panic, urgency, and action."

### Defending Australia's North: Lark, Gull and Sparrow Force (worksheet)
- **Lark Force (Rabaul, New Britain):** mainly the 2/22nd Battalion. Japan landed on 23 January 1942 and the Australians were swiftly defeated. "on 4 February, at least 130 soldiers were murdered by the Japanese". Prisoners being shipped to Hainan aboard the *Montevideo Maru* died when it was torpedoed by the USS Sturgeon: "none of the between 1,050 and 1,053 prisoners survived".
- **Gull Force (Ambon):** 1,100 troops sent to defend Laha airfield. Japan invaded on 30 January 1942 and Gull Force surrendered on 3 February. In the Laha Massacre, over 300 Australian and Dutch POWs were killed, with "zero survivors". POWs then endured starvation, beatings and disease at Tan Toey camp. "Of the 532 Australians left imprisoned on Ambon, only 123 were still alive... Nearly 80% of the force perished in captivity", a death rate second only to Sandakan.
- **Sparrow Force (Timor):** about 1,850 troops (2/40th Battalion and the 2/2nd Independent Company commandos). Japan attacked on 19/20 February 1942 with 20,000+ troops, and Lt Col Leggatt surrendered the main force. The commandos waged a guerrilla war for nearly a year with Timorese support. Churchill: "They alone did not surrender." About 150 died in the guerrilla fighting and about 340 Sparrow Force members did not survive the war. Between 40,000 and 70,000 Timorese civilians died.

### Japanese POW camps and the Thai-Burma Railway
- Death rate in Japanese POW camps was "27% as opposed to 4%" in German and Italian camps.
- 1929 Geneva Convention standards: humane treatment; food, shelter and medical care; safe, non-military work; communication (letters, Red Cross); protection from arbitrary punishment; Red Cross inspections.
  - Question: "Did the Japanese treat POWs in accordance with the Geneva Convention? Justify your answer."
- Railway facts (worksheet):
  - 415 km long, Ban Pong (Thailand) to Thanbyuzayat (Burma). Built because shipping by sea was too dangerous after Midway.
  - About 60,000 Allied POWs, around 13,000 of them Australians.
  - "Hellfire Pass" (Konyu Cutting). The "Speedo" period in mid-1943 meant shifts of up to 18 hours. 68 men were beaten to death at Hellfire Pass alone (Capt Reg Newton).
  - Rations: "a cup of watery rice gruel". Diseases: cholera, dysentery, malaria, tropical ulcers.
  - Mateship; Lt Col Edward "Weary" Dunlop built bamboo hospitals and stood up to guards.
  - Completed October 1943. "over 16,000 Allied POWs had died, including more than 2,800 Australians". 75,000–90,000 Asian labourers died. Known as the "Death Railway".
- Model paragraph: "A major challenge faced by Australian prisoners of war on the Thai-Burma Railway was the combination of severe starvation and tropical diseases. Involving incredibly small food rations of watery rice and a lack of clean water, these harsh conditions made the soldiers extremely weak and unable to fight off serious jungle infections... F Force experienced a devastating overall death rate of approximately 44%."

### Bombing of Darwin and Kokoda
- Darwin was bombed from 19 February 1942 to 12 November 1943.
- Kokoda: "The Australians created a defensive line from Port Moresby to Buna, known as the Kokoda Trail."
- Conditions to describe: disease, weather, terrain, insects, food supplies. Know the role of the "Fuzzy Wuzzy Angels" (Papuan carriers).
- Model paragraph: "One of the most significant challenges faced by Australian and Papuan soldiers on the Kokoda Trail was the extreme mountain terrain which comprised the landscape of inland PNG. Involving steep, muddy ridges and dense jungle, this unforgiving environment physically exhausted the troops and made transporting vital food and medical supplies nearly impossible. This extreme difficulty is exemplified by the brutal climbs over locations like that of the Owen Stanley Range which led to a severe lack of provisions due to the tremendous logistical challenges. The extreme terrain resulted in high casualty rates due to the inability of units to be supplied sufficiently with medical supplies, ammunition and a lack of ability to evacuate wounded soldiers."
- Paragraph scaffold ("bubbling"): definition of the challenge → description of its effect → specific example from sources → explanation of its impact. Sentence starters: "Refers to, involving, exemplified by, leading to, culminating in".

## PDHPE (Yearly Exam Thu 19 Nov, P5, 25%)
Format: Part A multiple choice (15 marks); Part B short answer (25); Part C extended response (10). It covers "All content covered in Sexual Health, Drugs, Nutrition and Fitness Unit".

### Fitness: Getting Active for Good
- "Physical activity: Any movement that expends energy"
- "Exercise: A type of physical activity that is planned and structured and that includes repetitive body movements"
- "Physical Fitness: The body's ability to perform physical activity for work, leisure and day to day activities"
- Barrier to exercise: "something that limits, prevents or demotivates an individual to participate in physical activity." Reasons people exercise can be environmental, family/cultural, social or economic.
- Guidelines for young people (13–17): "At least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous physical activity" every day; muscle- and bone-strengthening activity 3 days a week; a maximum of 2 hours of entertainment screen time a day; break up long periods of sitting.
- **Health-related components (5):** cardiorespiratory endurance, flexibility, muscular strength, muscular endurance, body composition. They are "linked to personal health, lifestyle and illness prevention".
  - Cardiorespiratory endurance: "the way in which the heart, lungs and blood vessels support the body during exercise through delivering oxygen to each working muscle"
  - Muscular strength: "the ability of a muscle or muscle groups to exert maximum force in a single effort"
  - Muscular endurance: "the muscle's ability to repeatedly contract for extended periods without tiring"
  - Flexibility: "the range of motion of the joints in the body"
  - Body composition: "an individual's total body fat in relation to their lean body mass (muscles, bones, tendons)". Typical ranges: boys 15–20%, girls 20–25%.
- **Skill-related components (6):** coordination, reaction time, power, agility, speed, balance.
  - Speed: "the ability for the whole body or part of the body to get from one point to another quickly". Slow-twitch fibres contract slowly but work for long periods; fast-twitch fibres contract quickly but tire faster.
  - Reaction time: "how quickly the brain can respond to a stimulus and react accordingly" (the ruler drop test)
  - Agility: "the ability to change direction and speed mid-motion in an efficient manner"
  - Balance: "the ability of an individual to remain still and centred"
  - Coordination: "the ability of the body to efficiently use different muscle groups together to perform larger movement patterns"
  - Power: "relates to both speed and strength... perform a movement both quickly and with a great deal of force"
- Benefits of physical activity (exemplar): maintains a healthy weight, boosts self-esteem, enhances mental well-being (endorphins), reduces the risk of chronic disease (cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, some cancers), improves cardiovascular fitness. Benefits can be physical, mental, social and spiritual.
- Paragraph structure: topic sentence → points ("this does that, leads to this") → wrap.

### Nutrition: A Healthier Me
- Influences on food choice: family/parents, peers, socioeconomic status/cost, media, culture/religion, individual attitudes, geographic location, access to foods, education levels.
- Australian Guidelines for Healthy Eating:
  1. Achieve and maintain a healthy weight: be active and match food intake to energy needs.
  2. Eat a wide variety from the 5 food groups: vegetables & legumes, fruit, grain/cereal foods, lean meats/poultry/fish/eggs/tofu/nuts/seeds, dairy.
  3. Limit saturated fat, added salt, added sugars and alcohol.
  4. Encourage breastfeeding.
  5. Prepare and store food safely.
- The six essential nutrients: carbohydrates (the primary fuel source; high-GI vs low-GI), protein (building blocks for growth and repair), fats (dense energy, insulation; saturated vs unsaturated), vitamins, minerals (calcium for bones and teeth, iron for oxygen transport) and water (the body is about 60% water; teens need 6–8 cups a day).
- "Macronutrients – Needed in large amounts – Carbohydrates, Protein, Fats – Provide the body with energy (measured in kilojoules)". "Micronutrients – Needed in small amounts – Vitamins, Minerals – Regulate body processes". **(This is the school's own micronutrient definition and also covers the Food Tech gap.)**
- Recommended daily serves: fruit 2; vegetables 5–5½; grains 6–7; dairy 3½; meat & alternatives 2½.
- Hand guide: palm = protein; cupped hand = grains; fist = fruit/vegetables; thumb = fats.
- Food labels: "the ingredient listed first is the one present in the largest amount". Aim low for saturated fat and sodium, aim high for fibre.
- Dehydration signs: dizziness, muscle cramps, nausea, difficulty concentrating, dark urine, dry mouth. Boys aged 14–18 need 1.9 L a day.
- Obesity facts: 1 in 4 children and adolescents and 2 in 3 adults live with overweight or obesity.

### Drugs and Addiction
- Most common illicit drugs (2019): cannabis, followed by ecstasy, misuse of pharmaceuticals, then cocaine. About 3.4 million Australians used an illicit drug in the past 12 months.
- Impacts on the individual: mental health issues, brain damage, cardiovascular and respiratory problems, stroke, seizures, hypertension, sleep disorders. Costs include financial strain on families, treatment, loss of income and homelessness. Governments spent about $1.7bn (2009–10): 64% law enforcement, 22% treatment, 9.7% prevention, 2.2% harm reduction.
- **Levels of prevention:**
  - Primary "addresses the causes... strategies to protect people from developing alcohol or other drug related problems" (education about harms, laws, role models, safe environments).
  - Secondary "promotes safety for those who have already used... higher or specific risk" (drug education, pill testing at festivals, prescription monitoring, random drug testing, clean needles).
  - Tertiary "help people with ongoing alcohol or other drug problems... overcome them or improve their quality of life" (detox, CBT, pharmacotherapy, 12-step programs, residential rehab).
- Why people use drugs: risk taking, predisposition, experimentation, gender and age, hedonism (the "buzz"), peer group, availability.
- Alcohol: increases risk-taking such as drink driving and unsafe sex; know standard drinks and BAC 0.05; "There is no safe level of drinking".
- Vaping: "the act of using an e-cigarette... liquids... typically containing nicotine, artificial flavours and various chemicals" heated into an aerosol.
  - 14.5% of 14–17 year-olds vaped in 2023.
  - Appeal: novelty, family or friend influence, the false belief that it is harmless, flavours, cheap and accessible.
  - Harmful compounds: formaldehyde, propylene glycol, nicotine (harms the developing brain).
  - Short-term effects: nausea, vomiting, airway irritation, chest pain, palpitations. It is a "gateway" to smoking.
  - It is illegal to buy nicotine vapes without a prescription.
  - Getting help: a doctor, counsellors, replacing cravings with exercise, educating mates.
- Other lessons: addictive behaviours, marketing and drug use, gambling.

### Sexual Health
- Terms to define (half-yearly revision list): abstinence, contraception, conception, sexual consent, STI, fertilisation, ejaculation, sexuality, sexual orientation, menstruation, homosexual, heterosexual.
- "Contraception – Preventing pregnancy by interfering with the normal process of ovulation, fertilisation, or implantation." Contraceptives fall into 4 categories: barrier, hormonal, IUD, natural methods.
- Male condom: lowers the risk of STIs, cheap, no prescription needed; but single use, can break, latex allergy. Pill: very effective if taken correctly, regulates periods; but no STI protection, must be taken daily, needs a prescription. Withdrawal: free; but not effective and no STI protection. Using no method is only "about 15% effective".
- Extended response practice: "Evaluate three different forms of Contraception" (description, advantages, disadvantages, effectiveness, availability, cost).
- STIs: "infections caused by bacteria, viruses, or parasites, passed through sexual contact. Some STIs are treatable, while others can be managed but not cured."
  - Common STIs: chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, herpes, HIV, HPV. Untreated STIs can lead to infertility or cancer.
  - Risk factors: unprotected sex, multiple partners, no testing, alcohol or drugs, poor communication.
  - Protective behaviours: abstinence or delay, condoms or dental dams every time, regular testing, HPV vaccine and PrEP, open communication, not sharing needles.
  - True/false facts: you can't tell by looking; you can get an STI from oral sex; condoms are mostly but not 100% protective; there may be no symptoms; you can get one the first time.
- Consent: "Consent is when one person agrees to or gives permission to another person to do something. It means agreeing to an action based on your knowledge of what that action involves, its likely consequences and having the option of saying no." (Family Planning NSW)
  - The age of consent in NSW is 16. A person in a position of care or authority cannot have sex with someone in their care.
  - "The only way to know for sure if someone has given consent is if they tell you". People can change their mind at any time. An intoxicated person cannot give consent.
  - Sexual harassment: "unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature".
- Other lessons: understanding sexuality, sexual orientation, relationships, cyber safety.
